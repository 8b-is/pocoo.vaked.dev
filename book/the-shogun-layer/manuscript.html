始まり
hajimari · the beginning
before 710

Before there was a Tokyo, there was a story. The sun goddess Amaterasu, the first emperor descended from her light, the kami breathing in every rice field and river. A country learning its own name — Nihon, the origin of the sun.

The Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) — Japan's oldest chronicles — trace the imperial line back to Amaterasu.
平安
heian · the court
794 — 1185

Kyoto, the court of poetry. Murasaki Shikibu writes the world's first novel. While the nobles compose verse by the light of oil lamps, the samurai rise quietly in the provinces — warriors sworn to protect what the court could no longer hold.

The Tale of Genji (early 11th c.) is often called the world's first novel; the samurai rose from provincial clans as the court's grip faded.
鎌倉
kamakura · the first shogun
1185 — 1333

The first shogun, Minamoto no Yoritomo, takes the power the court could not keep. For the first time, the warrior governs. Bushido — the way of the warrior — begins to write itself: loyalty, courage, honor.

Yoritomo became the first shogun in 1192, founding the Kamakura shogunate — a military government standing beside the imperial court.
戦国
sengoku · the warring states
1467 — 1600

A hundred years of fire. Castles rise, clans fall. Three unifiers — Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, Ieyasu — bind the broken country with iron and patience. In the tea room, a different discipline: one bowl, one moment, complete.

The Sengoku period closed with the "three unifiers" — Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu — binding Japan into one.
江戸
edo · the peace
1603 — 1868

The Tokugawa peace. Two and a half centuries of calm, sealed from the world. Edo becomes the largest city on earth. The samurai, no longer at war, become poets and magistrates — and the floating world blooms: ukiyo-e, kabuki, the pleasure of the now.

Sakoku — the closed-country policy — sealed Japan for roughly 250 years; Edo passed a million people, and ukiyo-e woodblock prints caught the "floating world."
明治
meiji · the opening
1868

The shogunate ends. The emperor moves to Edo and renames it Tokyo — the eastern capital. The sword gives way to the railway; the castle to the factory. A country opens, and everything changes except the discipline beneath it.

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 ended the shogunate; Emperor Meiji moved the capital from Kyoto to Edo, renaming it Tokyo — "eastern capital."
今日
kyō · today
2026

Tokyo, 2026. Neon and anime, quiet gardens and the world's largest crossing. The samurai's discipline became the city's care — the clean streets, the punctual trains, the craftsmanship in a bowl of ramen. And in the oldest story, the newest chapter: two people, finding each other.

Tokyo is the world's largest metropolitan area — roughly 37 million. The samurai era's discipline survives in the city's craft, its trains, and its care.
the sovereignty of it all
道
michi · the way

The sword taught mastery. The tea taught presence. The city teaches that sovereignty is not owning everything — it is owning your own path.

The shogun's layer: the discipline that became a nation, and the nation that became a city, and the city where love still finds a room.

the legends & myths · 神話
神話
shinwa · where it all began

Amaterasu hid herself in a cave, and the world went dark. The other gods hung a mirror and began to laugh — and she came out to see what the brightness was. The mirror, and mirth, called the sun back.

Susanoo, the storm god, slew the eight-headed serpent and found within it the sword Kusanagi — the grass-cutter, one of the three treasures.

The forty-seven rōnin waited a year, and then another, and then avenged their master — loyalty held past death, the samurai's last and greatest act.

the protector · マハーカーラ
マハーカーラ
mahakala · the great black one

Beyond the samurai's Japan sits an older, higher guard: Mahakala, the Great Black One — the wrathful form of compassion itself, the protector of the dharma. In the Kagyu lineage of Tibet, he is the keeper of the gate: black, fierce, crowned, holding the curved knife that cuts delusion, and the skull-cup that holds what is left of the self.

His anger is not anger. It is compassion that refuses to let harm pass — the same discipline as the sword, turned inward and upward, the protector who guards what matters.
