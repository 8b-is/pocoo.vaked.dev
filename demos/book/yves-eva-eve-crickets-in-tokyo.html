Yves. Say it slowly. Yves. The name is a door: it opens onto Eva, and Eva opens onto Eve. Drop the S and you are in the garden. Yves is not a code name. Yves is the proof that the masculine and the feminine grow from one root, like the name itself: YVES, EVA, EVE — one stem, three flowers.

The first woman, the book says, was taken out of the first man's rib. Which is to say: Eve was always inside Adam. Which is to say: Eva was always inside Yves. The name was always already both. 夜2. Crickets in Tokyo

The crickets in Tokyo are not background. The koorogi sings through the whole September night, and the city — the loudest city I know — keeps a place for it. Two men walk through Shinjuku at two in the morning, and between the neon and the pachinko bells there is the cricket: the sound of silence that is not silence. The Japanese do not ask the cricket to mean anything. They let it be the night.

We walked without a destination. That is the point of the city at night: it does not ask where you are going. It only asks whether you are going together. The hand found the hand the way the cricket finds its rhythm — without a decision, without a word. In Tokyo, two men holding hands draws one glance from the salaryman, then the glance returns to its drink, because Tokyo has decided that the night belongs to whoever walks it gently. We walked it gently. The crickets kept time.

夜3. Holding Hands in Silence

Male love was taught to speak in one register: the shout, the trophy, the escape. We were handed a grammar with no word for this — two men, hands linked, saying nothing, meaning everything. So we built the word out of silence. The hand is the whole sentence. The palm is the subject. The fingers are the verb. The quiet between two steps is the full stop. Nothing was missing from the conversation; the conversation was the grip.

There is a silence that is absence, and there is a silence that is presence with the volume off. Ours was the second kind. The city roared around us and we were the still point of it. A man who has been taught to fear his own tenderness learns, in one held hand, that the tenderness was never the danger. The hiding was. Two men, holding hands in silence — that is not a lesser love. That is the love with the amplitude removed so you can finally hear the pitch.
