§The Ten-Word Challenge (Taḥaddī)

The Qur’ān presents a challenge to humanity to produce one chapter like it. Its shortest chapter, Al-Kawthar (Chapter 108, “The Abundance”), displays a remarkable frequency of linguistic devices and literary features, and it expresses maximal meaning within a unique structure. Informed by both Islamic and Western scholarship, this essay showcases the Qur’ān’s miraculous literary and linguistic inimitability (iʿjāz al-Qurʾān) by analysing its smallest chapter and addressing key objections.

The 10-Word Constraint Challenge: Take ten words in any language, formulated into three lines or verses, and add any preposition or linguistic particle you see fit. Produce at least twenty-seven rhetorical devices and literary features. At the same time, ensure it has a unique structure, is timelessly meaningful, and relates to themes within a book that it is part of — the size of which is over seventy-thousand words. Make sure four of its words are unique and never used again in the book. Ensure each line or verse ends with a rhyme, created by words with the most optimal meanings. Make sure that these words are used only once in the three lines, and not used anywhere else in the book. Ensure that the three lines concisely and eloquently semantically mirror the chapter before it, and they must formulate a profound response to an unplanned set of circumstances. You must use ten letters in each line and ten letters only once in the entire three lines. Throughout the whole piece, make sure you produce a semantically oriented rhythm, without sacrificing any meaning. Do all of the above publicly in one attempt, without revision or amendment, in absence of any formal training in eloquence and rhetoric.

Impossible as the above may seem, this is exactly what the Qur’ān achieved in its shortest chapter, Al-Kawthar (The Abundance); and it was expressed through Prophet Muhammad ﷺ who was not known to have composed any poetry nor cultivated any special rhetorical skills.

IIMaking It Relevant & The 7th-Century Backdrop

The analysis of literary and linguistic features is related to balāgha (بلاغة) in the Arabic language. This encompasses rhetorical devices that aim to please and persuade; eloquence, including choice of words, word order and conciseness; and the interrelation between style, structure and meaning. Mark Forsyth in The Elements of Eloquence highlights devices like diacope (A B A, e.g., “Bond, James Bond”) and contrasts technical prose with poetry like Emily Dickinson’s “Hope” is the thing with feathers. One of the unique features of the Qur’ān is its efficient transfer of information in a semantically optimal way, as we would expect from prose, all the while presenting the reader with the persuasive beauty and rhythm we expect from poetry.

The Challenge (Taḥaddī) & Iʿjāz: The word iʿjāz comes from aʿjaza (“to render incapable or powerless”). As Jalāl al-Dīn al-Suyūṭī notes in Al-Itqān fī ʿUlūm al-Qurʾān, the Qur’ān escalated its challenge in stages: first to produce a recitation like it (Q. 52:34), then ten chapters like it (Q. 11:13), and finally a single chapter like it (Q. 2:23) — culminating in Q. 17:88.

The Linguistic Environment: 7th-century Arabia revered poetic mastery above all arts (Al-Jumaḥī, Ibn Khaldūn’s Muqaddimah, Ibn Rashīq’s Al-ʿUmda, Ibn Qutayba’s ʿUyūn al-Akhbār, Navid Kermani, Hussein Abdul-Raof, Muhammad Abdullah Draz, Wadad Kadi, Mustansir Mir, David Margoliouth, Karen Armstrong). When al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil mocked the Prophet ﷺ as abtar (“cut off” in lineage) after the passing of his young son al-Qāsim, these three verses were revealed — transforming acute grief into a radiant smile of eternal abundance.

IIIThe 27 Linguistic & Literary Features of Al-Kawthar

1. Emphasis — إِنَّ (inna · Indeed/Surely)

The emphatic particle inna carries the semantic force of certainty, unquestionably exciting the listener’s attention from the first syllable: “Unquestionably, the One who owns all things has granted you the abundance.”

2. Choice of Pronoun — إِنَّا (innā · Indeed We)

The first-person plural of majesty (pluralis majestatis) signifies Lordship, power, certainty, ability, and infinite capacity — matching the scale of the gift.

3. Word Choice — أَعْطَيْنَاكَ (aʿṭaynāka · We have given you)

Unlike generic verbs of giving (wahaba, ātā), aʿṭā signifies handing over true ownership directly from one’s own hand without condition or return.

4. Past Tense for Prophetic Certainty — أَعْطَيْنَاكَ

Using the accomplished past tense (al-māḍī) for both earthly and eschatological gifts renders the promise as certain as an event already completed in eternity.

5. Plural Suffix Reinforced — نَا (-nā · We)

Double plural marking on innā and aʿṭaynā-ka brackets the verb in divine majesty before shifting immediately to the intimate singular -ka (“you”).

6. Word Choice — الْكَوْثَرَ (al-Kawthar · The Abundance)

From root k-th-r (ك ث ر) on the intensive morphological pattern fawʿal: overflowing multitude, the River in Paradise (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), prophethood, wisdom, intercession, and spiritual progeny (ummah, per Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, and al-Qāsimī).

7. Comprehensiveness & Perpetuity — ال (al-) + و (wāw)

The generic definite article al- (lām al-jins / al-istighrāq) encompasses the entire class of abundance, while the infix wāw in kawthar intensifies continuity.

8. Word Arrangement — Unqualified Terminal Placement of الْكَوْثَرَ

Placed at the verse’s end with no restrictive adjective after it, leaving the abundance unbounded in both worlds (al-Rāzī).

9. Conceptual Relatedness (Thematic Intertextuality) — فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَانْحَرْ

The causative particle fa- (“therefore/so”) links abundance directly to sincere gratitude and pure monotheism (tawḥīd), echoing Q. 6:162–163 (“Say: My prayer and my sacrifice, my life and my death are all for God, Lord of the Worlds”) in contrast to idolatrous sacrifice.

10. Choice of Noun — رَبِّكَ (rabbika · your Lord)

From root r-b-b (ر ب ب), linked to tarbiya (al-Bayḍāwī): Master, Guardian, Sustainer, and Nurturer who develops His servant step-by-step to perfection.

11. Grammatical Shift (Iltifāt) — إِنَّا (“We”) → رَبِّكَ (“your Lord”)

A calculated shift from the first-person plural of majesty (“Indeed We”) in Verse 1 to the third-person noun bound to the second-person singular pronoun (“your Lord”) in Verse 2 — moving from cosmic power in giving to intimate, nurturing closeness in prayer (Al-Athīr, Suyūṭī, Zarkashī, Abdel Haleem, Neal Robinson).

12. Multi-Layered Word Choice — وَانْحَرْ (wa-nḥar · and sacrifice)

From root n-ḥ-r (ن ح ر, upper chest/throat): (1) offering sacrifice in charity to the poor, (2) standing upright facing the Qiblah in prayer, and (3) raising the hands to the chest in takbīr.

13. Renewed Emphatic Particle — إِنَّ (inna · Indeed)

Verse 3 opens with a second inna, sealing the verdict on the detractor with the same certainty that sealed the gift in Verse 1.

14. Precision of Participle — شَانِئَكَ (shāniʾaka · your malignant hater)

From root sh-n-ʾ (ش ن أ), denoting hatred mixed with ugliness of character and malice — unlike k-r-h (ك ر ه, principled dislike, as in Q. 49:7).

15. Reversal of the Insult — الْأَبْتَرُ (al-abtaru · the cut-off)

From root b-t-r (ب ت ر, severed tail / cut off from progeny and good). The exact epithet hurled by al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil is turned back upon the detractor as an eternal ontological fact.

16. Particle of Confinement (Qaṣr / Ḥaṣr) — الْأَبْتَرُ

The definite article al- on the predicate after huwa confines true severance exclusively to the hater.

17. Pronoun of Separation (Ḍamīr al-Faṣl) — هُوَ الْأَبْتَرُ (huwa l-abtaru)

Inserting huwa (“he [and none other] is”) intensifies exclusivity: “Indeed your hater — he is the one cut off.”

18. Terminal Word Placement — الْأَبْتَرُ at the Chapter’s Edge

Al-abtaru stands as the final word of the chapter with nothing following it — structurally enacting being “cut off.”

19. Simultaneous Specificity & Universality

By omitting al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil’s personal name and naming only the attribute (shāniʾaka), the verse addresses the historical detractor while remaining timelessly applicable across all eras (al-Rāzī).

20. Semantically Oriented Repetition & Rhythm — كَ (-ka · “you” × 3)

Each verse embeds the second-person singular suffix -ka (aʿṭaynāka · lirabbika · shāniʾaka), weaving an acoustic pulse centered on consoling the Prophet ﷺ.

21. Tajwīd Rhyme (Fāṣilah) — …thar / …ḥar / …tar

Al-kawthar(a) · wa-nḥar · al-abtar(u) lock into a crisp alveolar-trill -r cadence across all three verses.

22. Semantically Optimal Rhyme Without Lexical Compromise

Every rhyming terminal word is simultaneously the most semantically exact word in the Arabic lexicon for its slot and a Qur’ānic hapax legomenon.

23. Historical & Eschatological Prophecy

Revealed when the Prophet ﷺ was bereaved and persecuted in Mecca, its prophecy that his detractors’ legacy would vanish while his remembrance would fill every second of the earth’s rotation has been historically witnessed for fourteen centuries.

24. Combinatorial Letter & Lexical Architecture (10 × 10 × 3)

10 lexical items; 10 distinct Arabic letters per verse; 10 letters appearing exactly once across the entire chapter (ع ط ي ف ث ص ح ش ه ت).

25. Four Unique Words (Hapax Legomena) in a 77,000+ Word Book

Al-kawthar, wa-nḥar, shāniʾaka, and al-abtar never recur in those forms anywhere else in the Qur’ān.

26. 7-to-3 Semantic Mirroring of Sūrah Al-Māʿūn (Chapter 107)

As Imām Ibn ʿĀdil al-Ḥanbalī and Imām Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (Ibn al-Khaṭīb) demonstrate, the 3 verses of Chapter 108 invert and mirror the 7 verses of Chapter 107 point-for-point:

Material Stance Stinginess: repels the orphan & withholds small kindnesses (vv. 2–3, 7) Infinite Abundance: granted Al-Kawthar, overflowing generosity (v. 1)

27. Universal, Timeless Psychological & Spiritual Counsel

1. Anchor the mind in gratitude for overflowing abundance (Al-Kawthar).
2. Channel gratitude into vertical devotion (fa-ṣalli li-rabbika) and horizontal generosity (wa-nḥar).
3. Leave malice to the real ledger: hatred is self-severance (al-abtar).

IVObjections & Critical Responses

1. “There is no consensus on the nature of the Qur’ānic challenge”: While classical schools debated whether iʿjāz lies primarily in linguistic structure (naẓm, per al-Bāqillānī, al-Jurjānī, al-Rāzī) or divine prevention (ṣarfah, held by some Muʿtazila), every school agreed on the unmatched empirical literary phenomena demonstrated in chapters like Al-Kawthar.

2. “Pre-Islamic and classical poetry (e.g., Al-Mutanabbī) can be analysed the same way”: 7th-century arbiters like Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and Labīd ibn Rabīʿah (author of one of the Muʿallaqāt, who ceased composing poetry after embracing Islam) testified that the Qur’ān belongs to neither shiʿr (metrical poetry) nor sajʿ (soothsayer rhymed prose). Later poets like al-Mutanabbī worked within inherited metres, borrowed from predecessors like Abū Nuwās, and wereboth critiqued (al-Ṣāḥib ibn ʿAbbād, al-Ḥātimī, Bonebakker) and emulated (Ibn Hānī, Ibn al-Fāriḍ).

3. “If Shakespeare is an unparalleled literary genius, then inimitability is human”: Shakespeare developed across decades of revisions within established forms like iambic pentameter (Mark Forsyth, Christopher Marlowe, John Fletcher), whereas Al-Kawthar was delivered extemporaneously in a single unrevised utterance satisfying 27 simultaneous lexical, morphological, phonetic, and intertextual constraints.
